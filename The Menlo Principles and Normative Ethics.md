* The Menlo Principles extend the traditional ethical standards of the Belmont Report, originally designed for biomedical research, to the domain of Information and Communication Technology (ICT). 
* This shift is necessary because ICT research fundamentally alters the relationship between researchers and subjects: interactions are dispersed, indirect, and often invisible. 
* Data is collected at scale, frequently without individuals’ awareness, and the harms are diffuse and difficult to predict. 
* The Menlo Principles thus aim to provide a normative framework that guides researchers, oversight bodies, and funding agencies confronting these distinctive challenges.

* The framework consists of four principles: **Respect for Persons**, **Beneficence**, **Justice**, and **Respect for Law and Public Interest**. (Slides, Week 9)
* Each maps onto major traditions in normative ethics. 
* _Respect for Persons_ reflects **deontological ethics**, especially Kant’s insistence that individuals be treated as autonomous agents and never merely as means. (Reading 1, Week 8)
* In ICT research, this requires informed consent, respect for dignity, and special protection for those with diminished autonomy. 
* No matter how beneficial a research outcome may be, it is impermissible if it violates autonomy.

* _Beneficence_, by contrast, aligns with **utilitarianism**, emphasising the maximisation of benefits and minimisation of harms. 
* It encourages researchers to consider the consequences of their actions, perform cost–benefit analyses, and pursue social good. 
* The tension between these principles illustrates that Menlo is intentionally **pluralistic**: beneficent outcomes cannot override deontological constraints.

* The **Encore case** effectively demonstrates why this pluralistic structure is essential. 
* Encore researchers deployed code that covertly used users’ web browsers to measure censorship across countries. (Slides, Week 9)
* Their justification rested on Beneficence—they aimed to fight global censorship, a socially valuable goal. 
* However, their method violated _Respect for Persons_: users were not informed or consenting, and their computational resources were exploited without permission.
* As the SIGCOMM review later noted, this exposed users to “an appreciable risk of substantial harm.” (Reading 1, Week 8)

* A purely utilitarian framework might have endorsed Encore, focusing on the potential global benefits. 
* But the Menlo Principles reject such reasoning because deontological constraints prohibit treating people merely as instruments for research. 
* The controversy also revealed the inadequacy of older ethics boards that applied a narrow biomedical understanding of harm. (Reading 1, Week 8)
* In ICT contexts, autonomy violations and misuse of digital resources constitute genuine ethical harms even without physical risk.

* Thus, the Menlo Principles serve as a balanced ethical architecture for ICT research—one that requires researchers to weigh competing moral considerations and ensures that no single moral theory dominates at the expense of human dignity.

## Menlo Principles

Extends traditional ethical principles to **Information and Communication Technology (ICT)** research
* Adaptation of **Belmont Report** created for biomedical and human-subjects research
* **Why?** ICT researchers and subject relationship is disconnected and dispersed, data proliferation is a major issue
* Principles designed for broad audience, including ICT researchers, oversight bodies, funding agencies and others involved in digital health research

- **Respect for Persons** - participation is voluntary, based on informed consent
	- Treat individuals as **autonomous agents**
	* Protect those with diminished autonomy eg. children, those with severe cognitive impairments
* **Beneficence** - maximise benefits minimise harm
	* Consequentialist ethical theories focusing on **outcomes** of actions (utilitarianism)
* **Justice** - fairness in distribution of **burdens and benefits** of research
	* Equitable and fair research subject selection
	* Fair allocation of burdens across all impacted individuals
	* Researchers must not disproportionately burden certain groups, such as the poor or prisoners, while excluding them from potential benefits without a valid scientific reason
* **Respect for Law and Public Interest** - transparency and accountability
	* Researchers should be transparent in methods and results, engage in legal due diligence and be accountable for their actions
