>Is the identity of a leader a social construct

* The identity of a leader is best understood not as an inherent personal trait but as a **social construct** emerging through interaction. (Reading 1, Week 5)
	* It is a process of mutual influence NOT property of a single person
* This perspective, grounded in **social constructionism**, directly challenges the “Great Man” theory, which assumes leadership is innate and resides within particular individuals. 

* **leadership identity construction** arises through a dynamic cycle of **claiming** and **granting**.  (Reading 1, Week 5)
* A leadership identity is claimed when an individual attempts to exert influence—for example, proposing a plan, suggesting a strategy, or mediating a conflict. 
* However, this claim only becomes meaningful when others **grant** the individual the right to lead. 
* Grants of leadership occur when others begin looking to that person for leadership, complying with their influence attempts, or appointing them to leader-like positions. 
* Without the grant, the leadership claim collapses. Thus, leadership exists only through social agreement.
* The story of William "Bill" Crawford, the janitor who was granted a leader identity by cadets after they discovered his Medal of Honor, serves as a powerful illustration of how grants transform perception and subsequent behavior, resulting in him being **socially constructed as a leader**. (Reading 1, Week 5)

* This dynamic is visible in everyday contexts such as a university group project. 
* A student who suggests a deadline has **claimed** a form of leadership, but if the group ignores the suggestion, no leadership identity is created. 
* Conversely, if another student steps in to resolve conflict and the group accepts their guidance, the group has **granted** them a leadership role, if only temporarily. 
* Over time, repeatedly granted claims lead participants to **internalise** their identities: some begin to see themselves as “leaders,” while others internalise the role of “followers.” (Reading 1, Week 5)

**Positive Organisational Scholarship (POS)**
* The concept of leadership as socially constructed aligns with principles from **Positive Organisational Scholarship (POS)**, which focuses on unlocking human potential and enabling positive dynamics within groups. (Slides, Week 5)
* Through **mindful engagement**—an iterative process of approach, action, and reflection—individuals recognise and interpret their lived experiences as either grants or opportunities to claim leadership

* Within the course, working on the group presentation for Waymo I experienced the mindful engagement framework
	* Approach - noticing we were struggling with getting started, I did some research on the structure of the presentation, and created placeholder slides for things I felt we should cover
	* Action - the group splits up work according to the structure upon some discussion
	* Reflection - I thought about how I moved the progress of the presentation forward, but some workload could be distributed better for next time, which I could take a more active role in assigning work earlier
	* Through the framework, I exerted a small instance of leadership behaviour, and my claim was granted - I turned this small moment of influence into development opportunity

* The class discussions within tutorials are another good example of continuous mindful engagement from the tutor's perspective of leadership I experienced this term
	* Tutors approach the tutorial with aim of facilitating more active class discussion
	* The action in class takes place through discussion questions, in class conversations with the tutor and with peers, and time to share our thoughts.
	* Tutors can reflect upon what worked, and what could be improved, such as greater engagement from the class and more example applications of the weekly theory, and apply it continuously throughout the term
	* This cycle shows that leadership in teaching emerges not from formal authority but from mindful, relational engagement with students.

* Leadership is not something individuals possess but something that arises **between** people. 
* Leadership identities are therefore **relational**, continually co-constructed through everyday interactions.
