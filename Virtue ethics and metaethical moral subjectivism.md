* Virtue Ethics and Moral Subjectivism present fundamentally different pictures of what morality is, and these differences make them largely incompatible. 
* Virtue Ethics, grounded in Aristotle and later interpreters, holds that ethics is about cultivating stable qualities of character—courage, honesty, generosity, fairness—that enable humans to achieve _eudaimonia_, or human flourishing. (Reading 1, Week 2)
* This framework assumes that there is an **objective human good**, an account of what it means to live well as a human being. 
* As Aristotle argues, flourishing comes from fulfilling our distinct function: living according to reason. Virtues are therefore objectively desirable traits that help us live well. 
* Moral Subjectivism, as explained by DeLapp, rejects this entirely. It claims that moral statements are true only relative to individual attitudes: “X is wrong” means “I personally disapprove of X.” 
* There are no objective moral facts, only personal preferences and feelings. Two people may hold contradictory moral beliefs and both can be right for them. (Reading 2, Week 8)

**Conflict**
* The conflict between the theories is direct. 
* If Subjectivism is true, Virtue Ethics collapses, because the cultivation of virtue requires an independent standard of excellence to strive toward. 
* Without an objective _telos_—a human good—virtue becomes arbitrary. Subjectivism reduces the virtuous agent to a merely consistent one: whatever traits an individual approves of automatically become their “virtues"
	* This empties virtue of normative force. 
* Qualities like honesty or compassion have no moral priority over “selfishness” or “cruelty,” as long as a person subjectively endorses them. 
* Likewise, _eudaimonia_ becomes meaningless under Subjectivism. 
* Instead of the rich Aristotelian account of living a rational, flourishing life, flourishing becomes whatever an individual happens to prefer—effectively “feeling good,” not “being good.”

**Application to Professional Ethics**
* This incompatibility is especially evident in professional contexts. 
* Codes of ethics such as the ACM Code assume objective standards of conduct: responsibilities that computing professionals must uphold regardless of personal preference. 
* The ACM explicitly claims that the public good must be the primary consideration. (Reading 1, Week 2)
* A moral subjectivist could accept these duties only if they personally agreed with them, rendering the code optional, non-binding, and ultimately meaningless. 
* Professional virtue—rigour, honesty, respect for others—cannot be grounded in individual whim.

**How to Establish Dialogue**
* However, dialogue is still possible. A Virtue Ethicist and a Cultural Relativist, unlike a strict Subjectivist, can at least agree that virtues emerge from shared human practices. 
* They may disagree on whether virtues are objectively true or socially constructed, but they can collaborate on identifying the traits required for the flourishing of the computing profession. 
* The meta-ethical foundations diverge, yet the practical conclusions overlap: communities can still form codes that encourage the cultivation of professional virtue.
