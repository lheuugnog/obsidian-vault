
> How can procurement be ethical?

**Sustainable Procurement** - process of buying goods and services in a way that achieves the "most positive environmental, social and economic impacts possible over the entire life cycle" while striving to "minimise adverse impacts".

**Argument** 
* Ethical procurement is the practical expression of **deontological ethics** within organisational decision-making. 
* Rather than evaluating purchases purely through cost-benefit analysis, ethical procurement embeds duties, especially respect for human rights, environmental stewardship, and social equity, across the entire supply chain lifecycle. 
* Standards such as **ISO 20400:2017** formalise these duties, reflecting a principlist framework that recognises moral obligations that cannot be overridden by utilitarian efficiency alone.
* If, for example, a university purchases cheap laptops produced through forced labour, it violates core professional principles of serving the public interest and upholding dignity.
* Ethical procurement therefore reframes purchasing as a moral responsibility rather than a transactional function.

**Sustainable Procurement and SDGs**
* Sustainable procurement links closely to global sustainability goals, specifically **SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production)** and **SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth)**. (Slides, Week 7)
* Lewis (Slides, Week 7) emphasises that procurement has strategic influence: organisations shape their ethical and environmental footprint through what they buy. 
* Sustainable procurement aims to maximise positive social, environmental, and economic outcomes while minimising adverse impacts over the product’s entire life cycle. 
* This requires organisations to look beyond surface costs and consider hidden consequences embedded in global supply chains.

**ISO 20400:2017**
* **ISO 20400** operationalises principlism by embedding moral principles, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and respect for autonomy, into procurement processes.  (Slides, Week 5)
* The standard breaks sustainability into seven core subjects, including environmental protection, human rights, labour practices, and fair operating practices.  (Slides, Week 7)
* This ensures that ethical behaviour is integrated into decision-making procedures, not treated as an optional add-on. 
* Crucially, ISO 20400 foregrounds responsibility for **Scope 3 emissions**, which are generated not by the organisation itself but by its suppliers. 
* For many institutions, purchased goods and services constitute the majority of their carbon footprint, making ethical procurement the only effective mechanism to address these indirect impacts.

**Hidden Impacts of Tech**
* Technology supply chains illustrate the urgency of ethical procurement. 
* The environmental costs of IT: energy-intensive data centres, rare-earth mineral extraction, and rising levels of e-waste, are often hidden from consumers. 
* Moreover, the IT sector is a global hotspot for **modern slavery** -> sustainability includes social justice
	* Risks of human trafficking, forced labour and child labour in regions like SEA, India and China
	* Labour from cobalt mining, polysilicon production from Xinjiang, and electronic assembly lines.  (Slides, Week 7) -> clean energy sector needs to address
* Ethical procurement thus demands due diligence, transparency, and a willingness to reject suppliers who fail to uphold human rights.
* **UNSW’s Strategy:** UNSW’s specific framework focuses on reducing Scope 3 emissions (purchased goods), understanding impacts on nature, and focusing on circularity .


* Ultimately, procurement becomes ethical when organisations recognise that their purchasing power carries moral weight. 
* By aligning decisions with principled standards such as ISO 20400 and confronting hidden environmental and social harms, organisations can transform procurement from a cost-driven process into a vehicle for global responsibility.
