>Focus on uselessness of Ai ethics

* Munn’s critique of contemporary AI ethics is compelling precisely because it identifies a structural flaw: **abstract ethical principles do not meaningfully constrain the behaviour of powerful technology companies**. 
* However, the answer is not to abandon ethics altogether, but to shift from vague value statements to **operationalised, enforceable governance frameworks**. 
* Shneiderman’s **Human-Centred AI (HCAI)** model provides the practical alternative that Munn’s critique demands.

* Munn (Reading 2, Week 7) argues that modern AI ethics has become _“meaningless”_ because key principles such as fairness, privacy, and beneficence are so malleable that corporations can interpret them however they wish. As he notes, “commendable values like ‘fairness’ and ‘privacy’ break down when subjected to scrutiny,” producing multiple, incompatible visions. 
* Moreover, AI ethics is _“toothless”_ because these principles lack **mechanisms for enforcement**. 
* Instead of regulating AI systems, companies use high-level ethics frameworks as a reputation buffer—a form of **ethics washing**. 
* This allows organisations to appear responsible without altering business models, product design choices, or data extraction practices. 
* In this sense, Munn is right: abstract principles alone cannot mitigate the racial, social, and environmental harms of AI technologies.

* However, Munn stops short of proposing how ethics can be made effective. This is where Shneiderman’s (Reading 1, Week 4) HCAI framework directly responds. 
* Rather than relying on the hope that companies will voluntarily uphold ethical ideals, HCAI emphasises **engineering practices, organisational governance, and external accountability**. 
* Shneiderman argues that trustworthy AI emerges not from promises but from _“audit trails,” “analysis tools,”_ and clear procedures that assign **human responsibility**. 
* In this model, **reliability** is achieved through documentation of failures and near misses; **safety** is supported by internal review boards and public reporting; and **trustworthiness** depends on independent oversight bodies, professional standards, and government regulation. 
* Importantly, HCAI rejects the false dichotomy between automation and human control, asserting that systems should aim for **high levels of both**.

* Problem is NOT ethics itself but the FORM ethics currently takes. 
* When ethics remains abstract, it is easily ignored; when it becomes systematised through governance, monitoring, and enforceable standards, it directly addresses the “meaninglessness” and “toothlessness” that Munn critiques.
* To move from AI Ethics to **AI Safety**, we must replace symbolic principles with **testable, auditable, and enforceable** structures. 
* Only then can ethical commitments influence real-world AI systems rather than serve as corporate public relations.

**Argument:** Munn is correct that _abstract_ principles are useless. However, the solution is not abandoning ethics, but operationalizing it through Shneiderman’s **HCAI** framework. We must move from "Al Ethics" (philosophy) to "AI Safety" (engineering/governance), replacing vague promises with concrete **audit trails**.
- Munn argues AI ethics is "toothless" because principles lack the mechanisms to enforce compliance
- Shneiderman through HCAI defines a trustworthy system as one that _must_ have "audit trails," "internal review boards," and "independent oversight"
- Therefore, the problem isn't ethics itself, but the "meaningless" and "vague" _principles_ that Munn critiques -> we must move to a _testable, auditable, and enforceable_ model of governance.

In other words, companies can enjoy the appearance of ethics without the substance."
	- "Borrowing from the well-known concept of 'green washing,' this phenomenon of 'ethics washing' as a means of dodging regulation has risen to prominence in debates on AI ethics"

### Shneiderman's HCAI Framework (Reading 1a Week 4)

Shneiderman’s HCAI framework pivots away from the obsession with creating autonomous AI and instead provides a practical blueprint for systems that **amplify human control**.

* **Autonomy -> Control:** Shneiderman argues the traditional, one-dimensional view (more automation = less human control) is flawed
	* "high levels of human control AND high levels of automation" instead of "full autonomy"
	- "The new goal is to seek high levels of human control AND high levels of automation, which is more likely to produce computer applications that are Reliable, Safe & Trustworthy (RST)."

* **Governance over Principles** This directly answers Munn's "toothless" critique. Shneiderman's **Reliable, Safe & Trustworthy (RST)** systems are not built on abstract promises but on concrete, auditable governance structures. This is the "engineering/governance" solution your argument highlights.
	* Aim to be reliable via audit trails, incident reporting and independent oversight
- **Reliable (via Audit Trails):**
    - "Reliable... systems come from appropriate technical practices, which support human responsibility... fairness... and explainability... such as: **Audit trails and analysis tools to review failures & near misses**"
- **Safe (via Internal Oversight):**
    - "Cultures of safety... are cultivated by open management strategies such as leadership commitment to safety... **reviews of failures and near misses by internal review boards**... [and] **public reports of failures**to promote discussion"
- **Trustworthy (via Independent Oversight):**
    - "...raising trust by relying on respected **independent oversight structures**... including: ...professional organizations that develop effective voluntary guidelines and standards... **government agencies that regulate**... **accounting firms with demonstrated value in auditing**..."
