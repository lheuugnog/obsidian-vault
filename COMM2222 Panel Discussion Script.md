Hi everyone, I'm Hugo. I'm going to discuss two concrete experiences from my internship at **BT Financial Group**. My recent main project was as part of a team of six, writing a long-form strategic report on the **financial journeys of international students** and identifying opportunities for parent company Westpac. My role involved a lot of research, writing, and constant iteration based on supervisor feedback.

---

### 1. Concrete Experience #1: "The Silo and the Repetitive Report"

Concrete Experience:
    - I submitted my first major draft for my section, which was a **competitor analysis** of the international student financial landscape in other similar nations. 
    - Confident in research BUT feedback that information was good -> **section was repetitive.** 
    - Overlapped with what two other teammates had already written in their current market landscape and deep-dive sections.
    - My task was to go back, read through the entire report, identify every piece of redundant information in my section, remove it, and then find new information to fill the gaps.
    
For my reflective observation:
    - **What worked:** My research in isolation made sense and answered my section well. When going through the report, I kept a lot of the research and moved it to different sections. I also met the various deadlines allowing for timely feedback and opportunity for iteration.
    - **What didn't, and why:** My teamwork and communication was insufficient and I did my section mostly in a complete silo. The 'why' is that I was so focused on _my_ assigned task that I never stopped to communicate with my teammates about what _they_ were writing. I was viewing the report as six separate pieces of work, rather than one single, cohesive document.
    - **Align/Conflict:** This experience deeply conflicted with my past experiences doing group work at university. Generally, in past presentations and reports, I assigned or was assigned a section which I worked on, and then combined it all at the end before submitting. This experience showed me that in a corporate setting, being on a team means you are responsible for the _entire_ document's coherence, not just your one small piece. The experience and feedback **aligned** perfectly with the Australian Association of Graduate Employers survey from Week 7, which highlights **Teamwork** and **Communication Skills** as the top competencies employers assess, which I needed to work on

### 2. Concrete Experience #2: "Learning What a 'Reference' Really Is"

My 2nd concrete experience was one related to research and referencing
    - In my initial report drafts, I made a lot of claims to support my arguments, and backed these up with a link to a website or a news article. However, I made some arguments, like recommendations, based on my research without backing each one with evidence.
    - One of my supervisor's most significant pieces of feedback was that my referencing could be improved by backing up every single claim with a credible, citable source.
    - This meant I had to go _beyond_ websites and learn to find and use **academic journals, official government publications, and industry reports**—sources I had rarely used before. I had to spend hours digging through papers to find statistics and claims that could use to justify specific points in my report.

- Stage 2: Reflective Observation:
    - **What worked:** My initial arguments were the correct direction, and made sense to the supervisor.
    - Issue: My arguments had no **authority** and were essentially just my opinions. The 'why' is that I tried to move too fast with research, like finding the first stat I saw on a blog, and moving on. I was avoiding the 'deep' research because it was slow and I wasn't too familiar with using university databases or library resources for this kind of work.
    - **Align/Conflict:** This **conflicted** with my usual process of writing reports and researching, which focused too much on quick answers. This experience taught me that in a professional setting, research is about **building an rigorous case based on evidence**. This feedback gave me a clear area for improvement and was a really valuable skill I gained during the internship.

So, both of these experiences were helpful to move toward a more professional mindset, where others in the company look over your reports and research. The first taught me the importance of making my output well-integrated in a team, and the second taught me the importance of credible evidence in professional arguments.
