During my internship at BT Financial Group, I worked on a large project where we wrote a strategic report analysing the financial journeys of international students and identifying opportunities for parent company Westpac. Through the research, writing and constant iteration process, I identified two specific experiences I experienced and looked back on. One was the isolated ‘siloed’ nature of my work process, and the other involved managing more rigorous referencing standards. Through Kolb’s (1984) Experiential Learning Cycle, together with supervisor feedback, I was able to conceptualise my experience and aim to reshape my professional approach through Active Experimentation.

My first concrete experience involved my section of ‘competitor analysis’ without much integration with the work of my team, receiving feedback that there was significant repetition throughout the report. I realised with this experience that I viewed the report as a collection of individual assignments rather than a cohesive corporate report. As discussed in Week 7, "you do not rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems" (Clear 2018). Although I followed my first SMART goal of producing a polished client-facing report, I lacked a ‘system’ for effective collaboration in a professional workplace. Therein, I could work on my ‘system’ of forming a stronger ‘career identity’ throughout the internship, operating as a professional rather than a student (London 1983) where holistic collaboration rather than individual contribution is favoured. To address this, one of the discussion points in FFM2 was more active collaboration, building upon my SMART goal of improving professional communication and teamwork. The meeting gave me insight on collaborative processes used in the workplace, including scheduling a brief alignment meeting to map out overlaps before writing. In future projects, I will apply this by first scheduling a meeting and sharing drafts for team feedback, while focusing on team coherence and speaking up more if in doubt as my supervisor suggested.

My second experience feedback that my research and sources were insufficiently rigorous in providing authority to my arguments. This experience challenged my self-insight (London 1983) regarding my research capabilities. I assumed that finding words on the internet to back up my claim was sufficient, without recognising that the quality of the source dictated the value of the advice in a research report. By working on presenting stronger evidence, I could work on presenting a positive identity spiral (Day et al. 2009) where my analytical skills could develop with further reading and research. The experience taught me the importance of backing my case with strong evidence.

My supervisor’s feedback during the internship and the meeting made it clear to me that arguments were worth nothing without quality sources. FFM2 went over the importance of looking at academic material, as well as asking questions and targeting research to specific questions, in aligning with my first report-writing SMART goal. We went over applying these theories by going through other research reports to look at how arguments were structured and backed up. I will actively experiment with using journal articles and publications from libraries in future research and report-writing, integrating feedback from my supervisor on asking targeted questions.

My FFM2 with my supervisor was critical in changing my perspective from focusing on assigned tasks, to looking at how work fits in the team and company. The advice and suggestions can be applied to my two concrete experiences through Kolb’s cycle, moving beyond goal setting to building a strong professional identity.

Introduction 

During my internship at BT Financial Group within the wealth management division, I encountered challenges that tested my transition from a university student to a professional analyst. This reflection unpacks two key concrete experiences discussed in my panel interview: managing ambiguity in market reporting and navigating cross-functional team communication. By applying Kolb’s (1984) Experiential Learning Cycle and integrating insights from my Feedforward Meeting 2 (FFM2), I will demonstrate how my professional identity and systems have evolved.

Experience 1: Managing Ambiguity in Market Reporting My first concrete experience involved the difficulty of producing a broad-scope wealth management report without the specific rubric-style guidelines I was accustomed to at university.

Stage 3: Abstract Conceptualisation Reflecting on this through the lens of Identity (London, 1983), I realised I was defaulting to a "student identity"—waiting for permission and specific instruction—rather than adopting a "professional identity" of proactive inquiry. Course concepts on Identity Spirals (Day et al., 2009) suggest that hesitation can lead to a negative spiral of reduced confidence. I had assumed that asking clarifying questions might signal incompetence (a lack of Self-Efficacy), whereas in a professional setting, it actually signals engagement.

Stage 4: Active Experimentation To test this new understanding, I will apply my supervisor Fouad’s FFM2 advice to "speak up if in doubt" and "be curious." In future projects, I will implement a "scoping phase" within the first 24 hours of a task assignment. Instead of attempting to solve ambiguity alone, I will draft specific hypothesis questions to present to stakeholders immediately. This shifts my behaviour from reactive compliance to proactive definition of the task.

Experience 2: Cross-Functional Team Communication My second experience involved a lack of visibility when collaborating with the investments and superannuation teams, where I focused on completing tasks but failed to communicate progress effectively.

Stage 3: Abstract Conceptualisation I initially approached this with a goal-oriented mindset ("finish the report"). However, Week 7 course content highlighted the importance of Systems over Goals (Clear, 2018). I realised that while I had a goal to be collaborative, I lacked a "system" for communication. I was falling to the level of my systems, which were non-existent for external updates. This also relates to External Self-Awareness (Eurich, 2017); I was unaware that my silence was perceived as a lack of progress by the wider team.

Stage 4: Active Experimentation Integrating Fouad’s FFM2 feedback to "work hard and be collaborative," I will experiment with a new communication system: the "Proactive Status Update." Regardless of whether it is requested, I will send a brief Friday summary of work completed and roadblocks anticipated. This tests the theory that consistent, systematised communication builds trust and visibility, ensuring I am not just doing the work, but effectively integrating it into the wider team’s workflow.

Feedforward Meeting 2 Synthesis My FFM2 with Fouad Ngam was pivotal in confirming my Self-Insight (London, 1983). His specific instruction to "speak up" and "ask as many questions as possible" directly challenged my initial assumption that independence equalled competence. This feedback loop has shifted my focus for the remainder of my career development from technical perfectionism to relational curiosity. By combining this feedback with the Systemsframework, I am now better equipped to navigate the complexities of the finance industry with a sustainable, proactive professional identity.
