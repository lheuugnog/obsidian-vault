* The Moral Gambit is proposed as the most promising solution to the responsibility gap created by autonomous systems. 
* Traditional responsibility requires four conditions: **intentionality, causality, consequence, and choice** - yet unpredictable AI systems often break these links. (Slides, Week 8)
* Designers or operators may cause and choose to deploy a system, but they cannot intend or foresee the specific unintended outcomes produced by autonomous behaviour. 
* This lack of predictability and distributed agency creates a structural “responsibility gap.” 
* Attempts to close this gap through collective responsibility, hierarchical distribution, or faultless responsibility fail because they either dilute blame, rely on false assumptions (such as seniority correlating with technical understanding), or ignore intentionality altogether. (Reading 1, Week 9)

**Moral Gambit**
* The Moral Gambit reframes responsibility not as something assigned after harm occurs, but as something **voluntarily accepted beforehand**. 
* A human agent knowingly deploys an unpredictable system, taking a “gambit” (risk) that it will perform correctly, while accepting in advance full responsibility for all outcomes, including unforeseen ones. 
* In non-lethal contexts, such as logistics systems, cyber tools, or surveillance, this gambit can close the responsibility gap. The risks are reparable, and a human operator can ethically accept the consequences.

**Where it Fails**
* However, the gambit only works if the operator is **informed**. 
* A meaningful Moral Gambit requires “full awareness of risks,” “a high level of technical understanding,” and a supporting infrastructure involving traceability, process documentation, and interpretability.  (Slides, Week 8)
* If the AI is a **black box**, these prerequisites collapse. An operator cannot meaningfully accept responsibility for behaviours they cannot understand, anticipate, or trace. 
* Instead of a “voluntary” assumption of responsibility, the gambit becomes a reckless act based on ignorance. Thus, for any **opaque** system—even in non-lethal scenarios—the Moral Gambit becomes ethically invalid because the agent lacks the epistemic foundation to justify the risk.

**Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)**
* This ethical limit becomes absolute for **Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)**. 
* Even if an operator were fully informed, a gambit taken on human lives is impermissible. (Reading 1, Week 9)
* LAWS violate the **principle of distinction**, since unpredictability prevents operators from foreseeing whether non-combatants will be harmed. 
* They also violate the **moral equality of combatants**, who consent only to being intentionally targeted, not accidentally killed by unpredictable algorithms. 

* Therefore, the Moral Gambit may help close the responsibility gap for non-lethal, interpretable systems. But when systems are **opaque**—or when they **kill**—the gambit collapses.

### The "Responsibility Gap"

Traditional responsibility requires **Control** and **Intent**. With autonomous systems (like AI or Autonomous Weapons Systems - AWS), humans lack full control/intent. Therefore, nobody is responsible.
"fair" and "justified" ascriptions of moral responsibility require four conditions to be met (Slides Lec 2 Week 8)
1. **Intentionality Condition:** The agent _intended_ the outcome.
2. **Causality Condition:** The agent's action _caused_ the outcome.
3. **Consequence Condition:** The agent _understood_ and could _foresee_ the consequences.
4. **Choice Condition:** The agent _freely chose_ to act.
For an unpredictable AI, these conditions fail. The human designer/operator may satisfy _Causality_ and _Choice_ (they chose to build/deploy it), but they fail on _Intentionality_ and _Consequence_ for any unforeseen action the AI takes.
* "Problems with meeting these conditions underpin the 'responsibility gap'." (Reading 1 Week 9)
* "When considering AI systems, their behaviour may not result directly from the intentions of the individual designers, developers or deployers. This can be the case for two reasons: distributed actions and **lack of predictability**." (Reading 1 Week 9)

### What strategies address the gap?

**Strategy 1: Collective Moral Responsibility**
- Entire organisation (e.g., the military, the corporation) is responsible.
- **Why it fails:** This is "nominal" responsibility. It "risks creating scapegoats and makes moral responsibility less meaningful." (Reading 1 Week 9 ) It avoids _individual_ moral blame/praise.

**Strategy 2: Distributed Moral Responsibility (Chain of Command)**
- Distribute blame based on seniority, assuming those at the top have the most control and information.
- **Why it fails:** False assumption in tech -> high-level commander may lack the "high level of technical understanding" of the AWS, and "Tech-skills do not track seniority!" (Lecture 2 Week 8)

**Strategy 3: Distributed Faultless Moral Responsibility**
- Distribute blame to all agents based on their _contribution_ to the outcome, even if they had no "fault" or intent.
- **Why it fails:** Doesn't give _meaningful_ blame or praise because it ignores _intentionality_.

**Strategy 4 (The "Solution"): Meaningful Moral Responsibility via the Moral Gambit**

- This strategy reframes the problem. Responsibility is not _attributed_ by an outside party (which would be unfair), but _voluntarily accepted_ by the human agent _before_ the action.
	- Human agent *knowing* system is unpredictable makes a **"moral gambit"** (a risk) by choosing to deploy it. In doing so, they _voluntarily accept_ moral responsibility for _all_ outcomes, including the unpredictable ones.
- **Source:** `Week 8 Lecture 2.pdf` (Slides) & `Readings_Week_9_Lecture_1.pdf` (Taddeo & Blanchard).
- "...meaningful moral responsibly can only be discharged by human agents who are willing to take a **moral gambit**: they decide to design/develop/deploy AWS despite the uncertainty... hoping that unintended and unwanted or unforeseen outcomes may never occurs, but also **accepting to be held responsible if such outcomes will occur**." (Reading 1 Week 9)
- "The moral gambit overcomes this dilemma by shifting the focus from attributing moral responsibly to **accepting it on a voluntarily basis**." (Reading 1 Week 9)
### Moral Gambits in Non-Lethal vs. Lethal Scenarios

This is the most critical distinction in the reading. The "moral gambit" is only permissible for _non-lethal_ systems.

**Non-Lethal AWS (Permissible):**
- For non-lethal AWS (e.g., cyber-warfare tools, logistics, surveillance), the stakes are reparable (e.g., financial loss, property damage). A human agent _can_ ethically accept the risk for these outcomes.
- "In the context of national defence, when considering non-lethal AWS, the moral gambit **may be acceptable**." (Reading 1 Week 9)

**Lethal AWS (LAWS) (Impermissible):**
- A moral gambit for LAWS is unethical because the stake is human life, which is irreparable. A human agent _cannot_ ethically gamble with _someone else's_ life.
- "**This is not the case when considering LAWS**. In this case, the moral gambit would be a gambit taken on the lives of others. This is why... we argue that even if a human agent were willing to accept this gambit, it **should not be permitted and would remain morally unacceptable**." (Reading 1 Week 9)

A LAWS gambit fails because it violates core Just War principles, which are built on the four conditions: (Reading 1 Week 9)
1. **Failure of Consequence (re: Non-Combatants):** Unpredictability of LAWS cannot guarantee  **principle of distinction** (distinguishing combatants from non-combatants). An operator cannot foresee or control this consequence, making them unable to _justifiably_ take the gambit.
2. **Failure of Intentionality (re: Combatants):** Combatants only waive their right to be _intentionally_ killed. They do _not_ waive their right to not be killed by an _unintentional_, _unpredictable_ algorithmic error. The moral gambit is a gamble on an _unintended_ outcome, which violates the "moral equality of combatants."
